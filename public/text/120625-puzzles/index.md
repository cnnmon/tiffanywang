one of my favorite games is [the beginner's guide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Beginner%27s_Guide). made by the creator of iconic metanarrative [the stanley parable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Stanley_Parable), the game starts with narration by a fictionalized version of its actual creator, davey wreden, touring you through a series of unfinished games made by a friend named coda. instead of games with, you know, win conditions, they're more like surreal walking simulators. in fact, davey will near-immediately spoil the puzzles you do encounter. yet, its [metafiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metafiction) narrative will break your understanding of its world and make you reconsider exactly what you've been playing through the last few hours. i recommend it a lot, especially as someone who is of [two wolves](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/inside-you-there-are-two-wolves) (read: internally inconsistent) when it comes to the question of "why make art?".

when i tell people i like games, they're sometimes surprised that i'm really quite bad at call of duty or mario kart. rather, i enjoy puzzles; i grew up on a healthy dose of indie [point-and-click escape rooms](https://archive.org/details/adventure-jinx-minx-tower-escape) with unexplainable main characters, [town of salem](https://www.townofsalem.io/) social deduction puzzles, [deck-builders](https://dominion.games/), and [auto-battlers](https://teamfighttactics.leagueoflegends.com/en-us/). but i also liked games like the beginner's guide a lot. though, to me, it feels spiritually similar to a puzzle: it entices a worldview then breaks it apart.

any good puzzle designer is like a teacher. take [baba is you](https://store.steampowered.com/app/736260/Baba_Is_You/), a sokoban-style block-pusher game where you can chain rules together like "FLAG IS WIN"—so that if you touch the flag you will win the level. the game bounces between establishing its rules with easy tutorial levels and throwing you into the deep end. in the deep end, the next puzzle of the game should always be more _non-obvious_ than the previous; the first solution you try must never work. the puzzle designers use the tutorial levels to establish a certain expectation from the player and use it against them in the next level, where, if they try the same solution, they will push the block into some irretrievable corner or lose fantastically. the puzzle designer forces you outside the box to reimagine uses for their mechanics.

![baba is you](/text/120625-puzzles/baba.webp)

[portal](https://store.steampowered.com/app/400/Portal/) is another excellent teacher. first, it teaches you how to use something called a "portal" gun: shoot it on at any two walls and you can walk through the blue portal and come out the orange portal, or vice versa. it's a powerful mechanic! to keep the game from getting stale, the designers make use of walls you can't shoot portals onto. one partiuclarly memorable level makes you use portals to exploit gravity and fling yourself across the room. puzzles entice a specific worldview in one room, then break it apart in the next.

this is also, i think, why being wrong in a puzzle can feel so enjoyable. the [hypercorrection effect](https://psycnet.apa.org/doiLanding?doi=10.1037%2F0278-7393.27.6.1491) finds that the errors we made confidently are the ones we learn the most from—the shock to our system makes us pay attention. it also makes people more [curious](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0001284) about where they went wrong. so, the designer must be careful; if the challenge seems too hard, the player might quit or, worse, look up the solution. it's 90% of the job for puzzle designers to playtest their stuff with other people to account for the "average" player.

this made me think: what would puzzles look like if you could design for an individual? what would be a puzzle that is tailored to *you*?

at neurips 2025, i chatted with a posterer about _adversarial optimization algorithms_. this is the idea that you can train a game-playing agent (learner) using a second adversarial agent (baddie). say the learner is playing overcooked and begins to win games merely by moving around the map clockwise. baddie moves counter-clockwise, blocking learner in a corner until it can learn counter-clockwise navigation. interestingly, the adversary can't be *too* effective at sabotaging the task. it has to find a _rational adversarial example_, that is, a strategy that happens to poke holes in a specific weakness in the learner's policy. the ideal adversary is one that watches and adapts to its opponent's strategy.

that same neurips, i was exhibiting at the creative ai session my [moral dilemma tamagotchi](https://cnnmon.itch.io/principal). based in [moral foundations theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moral_foundations_theory), it's a virtual pet game wherein the pet learns and grows from your answers to moral dilemmas. the idea is that, as you keep feeding in advice, the pet will internalize earlier advice and call you out if you were being contradictory. incidentally, while exhibiting i noticed the pet (powered by fickle gpt-4o-mini) would be incredibly sensitive, sometimes incessantly arguing with their players for no reason at all. these conversational death spirals would require players to carefully rethink their wording or simply give up altogether, erasing their advice and writing "just do what you want". this,surprisingly, became a standout feature in people's minds.

![poster](/text/120625-puzzles/poster.jpg)

people don't expect ai, much less a tamagotchi, to fight back with their parent like a bratty teenager. in turn, the resistance became a form of play—where players were asked to re-assess their answers as their straightforward, obvious solution fell flat in front of them. it was a similar feeling one might get from puzzle games, but this time, the challenge was more personal: the pet was questioning _your words_ in specific.

in trying to embed language models into games, i spun myself out trying to [prompt away](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3544548.3581388) their numerous flaws and biases. yet, flaws persist; and sometimes that makes for an interesting game on both ends. for instance, players will often [try to jailbreak your system](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.01011) and sometimes succeed. the result is something like two imperfect models trying to poke holes in one another.

an obvious application for adversarial play seems to exist in education. [intelligent tutoring systems](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221414136_Toward_Measuring_and_Maintaining_the_Zone_of_Proximal_Development_in_Adaptive_Instructional_Systems) have historically worked to model a student's knowledge and keep challenges within their [zone of proximal development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zone_of_proximal_development)—a fancy way of saying their ideal puzzle difficulty. i'm genuinely optimistic for the future in which learning feels more playful and personalized.

i'm personally lingering around this question of: can we use games to make people experience new and interesting ways to be wrong? the moral dilemma tamagotchi was fundamentally a game about convincing someone of your opinion, and collected [a lot of diverse, interesting advice](https://huggingface.co/datasets/cnnmon/moral-dilemma-responses) as a result. can we use games to understand more about different people, and, in turn, teach them something new?
