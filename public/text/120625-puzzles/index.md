one of my favorite games is [the beginner's guide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Beginner%27s_Guide). made by the creator of iconic metanarrative [the stanley parable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Stanley_Parable), the game starts with narration by a fictionalized version of its actual creator, davey wreden, touring you through a series of unfinished games made by a friend named coda. instead of games with, you know, win conditions, they're more like surreal walking simulators. in fact, davey will near-immediately spoil the puzzles you do encounter. yet, its [metafiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metafiction) narrative will break your understanding of its world and make you reconsider exactly what you've been playing through the last few hours. i recommend it a lot, especially as someone who is of [two wolves](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/inside-you-there-are-two-wolves) (read: internally inconsistent) when it comes to the question of "why make art?".

when i tell people i like games, sometimes they're surprised i don't refer to any of the highest-selling triple-a or first-person shooter titles. rather, i enjoy puzzles. i grew up on indie [point-and-click escape rooms](https://archive.org/details/adventure-jinx-minx-tower-escape) with unexplainable main characters; social deduction games like [town of salem](https://www.townofsalem.io/) which developed a "right" way to take notes; [deck-builders](https://dominion.games/), [auto-battlers](https://teamfighttactics.leagueoflegends.com/en-us/), you name it. so when i played the beginner's guide, i was surprised that i enjoyed it so whwen it didn't fit cleanly into any of these archetypes. upon reflection, though, i believe i enjoyed it for the same reasons people enjoy puzzles.

let me explain: a good puzzle designer is a good teacher. let's look at [baba is you](https://store.steampowered.com/app/736260/Baba_Is_You/), a sokoban-style block-pusher game where you can chain rules together like "FLAG IS WIN"—so that if you touch the flag you will win the level. the game bounces between establishing its rules with easy tutorial levels and throwing you into the deep end. in the deep end, the next puzzle of the game should always be more _non-obvious_ than the previous; the first solution you try must never work. the puzzle designers use the tutorial levels to establish a certain expectation from the player and use it against them in the next level, where, if they try the same solution, they will push the block into some irretrievable corner or lose fantastically. the puzzle designer demands you to think outside the box and imagine new uses for its blocks, besides the ones they've already shown you.

[portal](https://store.steampowered.com/app/400/Portal/) is another infamous puzzle game that deceives its players. first, it teaches you how to use something called a "portal" gun: shoot it on at any two walls and you can walk through the blue portal and come out the orange portal, or vice versa. it's a powerful mechanic! to keep the game from getting stale, the designers make use of walls you can't shoot portals onto. one partiuclarly memorable level makes you use portals to exploit gravity and fling yourself across the room. puzzles entice a specific worldview in one room, then break it apart in the next.

research in metacognition suggests why this is so fun. the [hypercorrection effect](https://psycnet.apa.org/doiLanding?doi=10.1037%2F0278-7393.27.6.1491) which suggests that if we were first confident in our answer, discovering an error can make us more effective learners—and potentially more [curious](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11713883/) too. puzzle designers must design a level progression that is neither too hard nor too easy, but rather well-balanced at the edge of the player's capabilities. if the gap between what the player knows and what the puzzle demands is too large, the player might quit or, worse, _look up_ the solution. so, puzzle games represent this interesting interplay where the designer is always trying to one-up the player. traditionally, this is limited to designers imagining an "average" player. but what if there was a way to design more expressively, for a specific rather than an average?

at neurips 2025, i chatted with a posterer about _adversarial optimization algorithms_: the idea that you can train a game-playing agent (learner) using a second adversarial agent (baddie). say the learner is playing overcooked and begins to win games merely by moving around the map clockwise. baddie moves counter-clockwise, blocking learner in a corner until it can learn counter-clockwise navigation. interestingly, the adversary can't be too effective at sabotaging the task. it has to find a _rational adversarial example_, that is, a strategy that happens to poke holes in a specific weakness in the learner's policy. even with machines, the ideal adversary is one that watches its opponent's strategy and adapts accordingly.

at another [corner of neurips]((https://neurips.cc/Conferences/2025/CallForCreativeAI)), i was exhibiting my [moral dilemma tamagotchi](https://cnnmon.itch.io/principal)—based in [moral foundations theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moral_foundations_theory), the game consists of a virtual pet that learns and grows from your answers to moral dilemmas. incidentally, while exhibiting i noticed a prompt bug where the pet (powered by gpt-4o-mini) would sometimes incessantly argue with their players' advice. these conversational death spirals would require their player to edit the wording of their advice, add reasoning, or simply give up altogether and say "just do whatever you want". surprisingly, this bug became many people's highlight of the gameplay.

![poster](/text/120625-puzzles/poster.jpg)

i suspected that part of the reason was that people don't expect ai, much less a tamagotchi, to fight back with their parent like a bratty teenager. but i would argue this resistance also became a form of play, where players were asked to re-assess and improve their answer. their straightforward, obvious solution was rejected—like a puzzle game! but unlike static puzzle games, this challenge was personal. the pet was not questioning the average player. the pet was questioning _your words in specific_.

of course, language models have many of their own flaws and biases. using them in games means you're asking players to [try to jailbreak your system](https://huggingface.co/datasets/cnnmon/moral-dilemma-responses). the result is something like two imperfect models poking holes in one another. but i'm optimistic that this is an experience that (1) teaches people that they _can_ best technology and (2) leads to more personalized puzzle games, in the form of adversarial play.

the obvious application for adversarial play seems to exist in education. [intelligent tutoring systems](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221414136_Toward_Measuring_and_Maintaining_the_Zone_of_Proximal_Development_in_Adaptive_Instructional_Systems) have long worked to model a student's knowledge and keep challenges within their [zone of proximal development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zone_of_proximal_development). i'm optimistic for this future in which learning is more fun and effective.

however, i'm personally interested in testing something more subjective. like with the moral dilemma tamagotchi, i'm interested in the application of adversarial play to understand messy human systems where many truths may co-exist. can we use adversarial play to help people better understand the transitory nature of emotions or how governments work or why miscommunication occurs in conflict? can we design games around the multitude of perspectives we inhabit?

tl;dr: _reject games that are obvious and solvable upon first glance. make games where people may experience new and interesting ways to be wrong._
