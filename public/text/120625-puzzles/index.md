since childhood, i've loved puzzle games. the [point-and-click escape room video games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escape_room_video_game) that preceded any physical escape rooms; puzzle video games like [Portal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portal_(video_game)) and [Outer Wilds](https://store.steampowered.com/app/753640/Outer_Wilds/); now, puzzlehunts à la [MIT Mystery Hunt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIT_Mystery_Hunt) which can present clues as a [madlibs](https://puzzles.mit.edu/2015/puzzle/feeling_bluefin/) or [Magic-the-Gathering card](https://2018.galacticpuzzlehunt.com/puzzle/cryptic-command.html) or a [Choose Your Own Adventure](https://silphpuzzlehunt.com/puzzle/plaintext-adventure).

a puzzle tends to be as hard to define [as a game is](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_resemblance). when i say puzzles, what comes to mind is typically the New York Times games catalogue, where the puzzle format stays the same every time and you get (somewhat linearly) better at it. though, to me, the spirit of a puzzle is this: something that entices a specific worldview then breaks it apart.

in sequential puzzle games, any good designer is like a teacher. take [Baba Is You](https://store.steampowered.com/app/736260/Baba_Is_You/), a "sokoban"-style, block-pusher game where you may chain blocks to construct rules like "FLAG / IS / WIN". these rules then reverberate into the game's logic and makes it so that, if you touch the flag, you will indeed win. the game spends some time on setting up these mechanics before presenting you with new levels where you must throw out old, straightforward ways of doing things. an example is when the character, Baba, is trapped completely behind a wall. instead, the player must redefine the titular rule "BABA / IS / YOU".

[portal](https://store.steampowered.com/app/400/Portal/) is another excellent example. first, it teaches you how to shoot at any two walls such that you can walk from one portal out the other. designers place in walls you can't shoot portals onto. suddenly, you can't just portal across the room, but you must make do with gravity -- something that has always been in the game! -- to fling yourself across the room.

a puzzle's enjoyment seems to come from being wrong: you see a level and the solution seems easy, and then you run into an unexpected wall that makes you rethink your entire approach. Janet Metcalfe and Brady Butterfield defined a term called [hypercorrection effect](https://psycnet.apa.org/doiLanding?doi=10.1037%2F0278-7393.27.6.1491) in 2001, wherein people are more likely to correctly learn and [be curious about](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0001284) the right answer if they received feedback for a mistake they were highly confident in. the designer tip-toes between illusory effortlessness and surprising difficulty, because heaven forbid the player gives up or, worse, looks up the solution. the designer must arise a power within the player to overcome the challenge.

however, the "average player" is quite difficult to imagine; if you're a puzzle designer, you usually have to choose between satisfying beginner "puzzle-tasters" or your more dedicated puzzlers. others argue that gaps in difficulty are a failure of design, and you can combat this by providing in-game hints, solutions to  similar puzzles, or [even just ceremoniously giving the player the answer](https://cjleo.com/blog/some-puzzles-are-simply-impossible/) - which all come with their own tradeoffs. but what if we could leverage personalization to actually design a puzzle specifically tailored to the player?

at neurips 2025, i chatted with a posterer about _adversarial optimization algorithms_. this is the idea that you can train a game-playing agent (learner) using a second adversarial agent (baddie). say the learner is playing overcooked and begins to win games merely by moving around the map clockwise. baddie moves counter-clockwise, blocking learner in a corner until it can learn counter-clockwise navigation. interestingly, the adversary can't be *too* effective at sabotaging the task. it has to find a _rational adversarial example_, that is, a strategy that happens to poke holes in a specific weakness in the learner's policy. the ideal adversary is one that watches and adapts to its opponent's strategy.

i had also recently made and exhibited my [moral dilemma tamagotchi](https://cnnmon.itch.io/principal) game. the idea is that you're raising a virtual pet that learns and grows from your answers to moral dilemmas, and it will prod and question and poke holes to better understand what you mean. i was using gpt-4o-mini, which was especially fickle and sensitive to prompt instructions, and thus would trap players in conversational death spirals where the player would sometimes need to give up altogether (writing "i don't care anymore do whatever you want"). surprisingly, players commented this felt more realistic and compelling - ai pet as a bratty teenager.

![poster](/text/120625-puzzles/poster.jpg)

resistance can invite reflection by prompting people to [consider alternatives](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Mark-Lepper-2/publication/16669522_Considering_the_Opposite_A_Corrective_Strategy_for_Social_Judgment/links/56107d7508ae48337519fedb/Considering-the-Opposite-A-Corrective-Strategy-for-Social-Judgment.pdf) and [re-explain their understanding to themselves](https://shibboleth2.asu.edu/idp/profile/SAML2/Redirect/SSO?execution=e1s1). such challenges - when properly [communicating confidence in one's ability](https://psycnet.apa.org/doiLanding?doi=10.1037%2Fa0033906) - can motivate improvement more than [flattery](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9686450/).

an obvious application for adversarial play seems to exist in education. [intelligent tutoring systems](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221414136_Toward_Measuring_and_Maintaining_the_Zone_of_Proximal_Development_in_Adaptive_Instructional_Systems) have historically worked to model a student's knowledge and keep challenges within their [zone of proximal development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zone_of_proximal_development) - that is, their ideal puzzle difficulty. with language models, there may exist a future where learning feels more playfully adversarial - where we can use games to elicit confidence in one's ability to overcome anything.

as our digital environments are becoming personalized, i think designing them to be too agreeable and easy-to-consume is a bit lazy. there's a case for [designing for friction](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/130554/1/Design_Frictions_CHI2016LBW.pdf) and [discomfort](https://people.cs.nott.ac.uk/pszjm2/uploads/2012/11/Uncomfortable-interactions.pdf). it feels a bit reminiscent of the post-WWI [dada movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dada), where artists collected around making art that was absurd and irritating as political protest. i'm excited about a world where we can design new and interesting ways to be wrong, and reconsider.
