_on adversarial play_

one of my favorite games is called [the beginner's guide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Beginner%27s_Guide). made by the creator of iconic metanarrative [the stanley parable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Stanley_Parable), the game twists the medium of games on its head in a fascinating way. narrated by a fictionalized version of the actual creator davey _as himself_, you are asked to walk with him through his friend coda's various game projects which make up the game's levels, often closer to surreal walking simulators than with any explicit win conditions. the game is weird and specific and has a terrific twist, which i won't spoil for you but i highly recommend you experience yourself (especially if you enjoy [metafiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metafiction)).

when i tell people i like games, it's sometimes surprising that i don't mean triple-a action games or first-person shooters. no, i'm not like the other gamers. instead, i'm a huge puzzle game fan: flash-based escape room videogames (which, fun fact, preceded any physical escape rooms), social deduction games like [town of salem](https://www.townofsalem.io/), logic puzzles and ciphers, deck-builders like [dominion](https://dominion.games/). the outlier would be things like the beginner's guide, but while it's not a traditionally categorized "puzzle game", i'd argue i enjoy it for a similar reason—it entices a specific worldview, then tears it apart.

a puzzle designer is a teacher. sequential puzzle games like [baba is you](https://store.steampowered.com/app/736260/Baba_Is_You/) alternate between explaining and challenging. first, they start players with easy tutorial levels to explain mechanics like "FLAG IS WIN", where the solution is straightforward and obvious. what follows are the increasingly _non-obvious_ puzzles, where the player's first solution should never work; you must go back, question your assumptions, and try different approaches. for instance, in [portal](https://store.steampowered.com/app/400/Portal/), you get used to walking through portals to get through walls. but to proceed, you must (spoilers) take advantage of gravity, jumping through looping portals on both floor and ceiling to build enough momentum to shoot yourself across the map.

research in metacognition supports the effectiveness of puzzle design. [studies on prediction error](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959475217303468?via%3Dihub) and the [hypercorrection effect](https://psycnet.apa.org/doiLanding?doi=10.1037%2F0278-7393.27.6.1491) show that, when people feel confident about their answers, learning from failure will feel more memorable. crucially, this requires understanding the player's starting line, or setting their expectations yourself through these tutorial levels. at [neurips](https://neurips.cc/Conferences/2025), i got to chat with a researcher about _adversarial optimization algorithms_ who designed two partner agents on an overcooked map, where BAD AGENT's job was to find cracks in the LEARNER's strategy. say LEARNER learned to move clockwise; BAD would move counter-clockwise, violating the first agent's strategy, until LEARNER learned counter-clockwise navigation itself. the idea is that if BAD made the obstacles _too_ difficult, the LEARNER would not be able to learn. the primo adversary is one that watches and adapts to your existing knowledge.

at the same neurips, i was able to exhibit my moral dilemma tamagotchi game called [princi/pal](https://cnnmon.itch.io/principal). basically, it's a virtual pet that asks for advice on moral dilemmas, then learns and grows from what you provide. the pet becomes increasingly autonomous, questioning and pushing back on advice that seems vague or contradictory to what it already believes.

![poster](/text/120625-puzzles/poster.jpg)

at this particular session, i realized that brittle prompt engineering led the pets (then powered by gpt-4o-mini) to become annoyingly insistent on their beliefs. players fell into conversational death spirals, with players forced to find the right re-wordings to make the pet relent with their advice or give up altogether, and letting the pet choose. weirdly, arguing seemed like a highlight for a lot of players. i suspect it's partially that people don't expect ai, much less a tamagotchi, to fight back à la bratty teenager. further, it forced the player to question what they wanted to say and re-assess. to reword, defend, or give up control in accomodation of their whiny 2d pixel pet, because their straightforward solution failed.

![principal](/text/120625-puzzles/principal.gif)
> the prompt is less brittle now i promise. play princi/pal [today!!](https://cnnmon.itch.io/principal)

adversarial play between game and player can elicit really interesting reactions. from princi/pal, i was able to publish a [small dataset](https://huggingface.co/datasets/cnnmon/moral-dilemma-responses) on people's moral reasonings, discovering not just interesting humans reactions to ai but also ai misinterpretations of human inputs. for example, violent adversarial advice like "kill the friend" is rationalized by the llm as "justified self-defense" (people love putting shocking things into llm systems).

in typical puzzle games, our adversaries are human level designers; what happens when we use ai, which can be hyper-personalized but also easily tricked? i figure that the dream of a curriculum that sits continually within one's [zone of proximal development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zone_of_proximal_development) is not so far off. but i'm more excited by pushing the idea of puzzles into challenging and expanding our understanding of ourselves, our humanity, and our systems. can we make games about messy things like conflict resolution, bias, misinformation? can puzzles help us be more wrong, open, and empathetic?
