one of my favorite games is [the beginner's guide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Beginner%27s_Guide). made by the creator of iconic metanarrative [the stanley parable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Stanley_Parable), it twists the medium of games in a fascinating way. the game is narrated by a fictionalized version of its actual creator, davey wreden, who tours you through a series of unfinished games supposedly made by his friend coda. these turn out to be less like games with, you know, win conditions, and more like surreal walking simulators—in fact, davey will sometimes just tell you the answers to the puzzles you encounter. yet the game eventually breaks your understanding of both its world and its characters in a way that hits especially hard as someone who is of [two wolves](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/inside-you-there-are-two-wolves) when it comes to art (i also love [metafiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metafiction)).

when i tell people i like games, they're sometimes surprised that i never mean triple-a action titles or first-person shooters. instead, i'm a real puzzle gamer. i grew up on [point-and-click escape rooms](https://archive.org/details/adventure-jinx-minx-tower-escape), social deduction games like [town of salem](https://www.townofsalem.io/), [deck-builders](https://dominion.games/), [auto-battlers](https://teamfighttactics.leagueoflegends.com/en-us/). while the beginner's guide is not categorized as a traditional puzzle game, i enjoy it for a very similar reason: it entices you to a specific worldview, then tears it apart.

a good puzzle designer is like a teacher. [baba is you](https://store.steampowered.com/app/736260/Baba_Is_You/) is a great puzzle game where you can interact with programming-like logic blocks. it starts you off with easy tutorial levels that establish rules like "FLAG IS WIN," where the solution is straightforward and obvious. quickly, it ramps up into its _non-obvious_ puzzles. here, the first solution always fails (there must be some head-hitting to build up to a euphoric "a-ha" moment), and you must question your understanding of the game's rules and mechanics to move forward.

![baba is you](/text/120625-puzzles/baba.webp)
> tutorial level from [baba is you](https://store.steampowered.com/app/736260/Baba_Is_You/)

[portal](https://store.steampowered.com/app/400/Portal/) does this particularly well. you first learn the obvious affordance of portals: walk through a blue portal and come out the orange portal, and vice versa. later, you're introduced to walls you can't shoot portals on. you must exploit the existence of gravity itself, falling through looping portals to build enough momentum to fling yourself across the map. your understanding of the rules has expanded.

research in metacognition helps explain why this design can be so effective. [studies on prediction error](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959475217303468?via%3Dihub) and the [hypercorrection effect](https://psycnet.apa.org/doiLanding?doi=10.1037%2F0278-7393.27.6.1491) suggest that someone's error can become a particularly memorable learning experience if they were initially confident in their solution. to produce that kind of useful surprise, we can't just throw any old puzzles at the player, but only present stuff at the edge of the player's expectations (or set expectations yourself using tutorial levels). if the gap between what the player knows and what the puzzle demands is too large, the player might not even know where to start. players might start (gasp) looking up the solution.

i'm optimistic in how new technologies could flip puzzle design on its head. traditional level designers must anticipate how the *average* player might react (i.e. "most players will know or easily grasp how [sokoban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sokoban) mechanics work"). but what if we could personalize this experience—instead reacting to the player's unique perspective and presenting puzzles to challenge them?

at neurips 2025, i chatted with a posterer about _adversarial optimization algorithms_: the idea that you can train a game-playing agent (learner) using a second adversarial agent (baddie). say the learner is playing overcooked and begins to win games merely by moving around the map clockwise. baddie moves counter-clockwise, blocking learner in a corner until it can learn counter-clockwise navigation. interestingly, the adversary can't be too effective at sabotaging the task. it has to find a _rational adversarial example_, that is, a strategy that happens to poke holes in a specific weakness in the learner's policy. even with machines, the ideal adversary is one that watches its opponent's strategy and adapts accordingly.

at another [corner of neurips]((https://neurips.cc/Conferences/2025/CallForCreativeAI)), i exhibited my game [princi/pal](https://cnnmon.itch.io/principal) and watched people play. princi/pal is a moral dilemma tamagotchi; using a framework based in [moral foundations theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moral_foundations_theory), i simulated a virtual pet that learns and grows from your natural language answers to moral dilemmas. i noticed a bug in which, due to insufficiently controlling gpt-4o-mini, players fell into long arguments with their pets. they would either need to iteratively edit their advice to see when the pet might eventually relent, or gave up entirely (saying: "ok do whatever you want"). people found this surprising; and surprisingly, people enjoyed it.

![poster](/text/120625-puzzles/poster.jpg)

people don't expect ai, and much less a tamagotchi, to fight back like a bratty teenager. further, the resistance became a form of play: players were forced to re-assess their straightforward answer and instead question whether what they said was reasonable, consistent, or clear. their intuitive answer was rejected, much like in a puzzle game. but unlike a puzzle game, it was a personal attack; the pet was questioning your advice in specific.

the nature of digital adversaries is changing. previously, this required a puzzle designer manually prodding at the average player. now, that role may be able to be trasnformed by language models. of course, language models have many flaws of their own—they can be [jailbroken](https://huggingface.co/datasets/cnnmon/moral-dilemma-responses) easily (and boy do people love jailbreaking language models). the result looks something like two imperfect models poking holes at each other. however, there is also lots of potential here (if we can figure out how to wield it correctly) in creating experiences that challenge individual players in a more personalized way.

the obvious application for adversarial play seems to exist in education. [intelligent tutoring systems](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221414136_Toward_Measuring_and_Maintaining_the_Zone_of_Proximal_Development_in_Adaptive_Instructional_Systems) have long worked to model a student's knowledge and keep challenges within their [zone of proximal development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zone_of_proximal_development). i'm optimistic for this future in which learning is more fun and effective.

however, i'm personally interested in testing something more subjective. like with the moral dilemma tamagotchi, i'm interested in the application of adversarial play to understand messy human systems where many truths may co-exist. can we use adversarial play to help people better understand the transitory nature of emotions or how governments work or why miscommunication occurs in conflict? can we design games around the multitude of perspectives we inhabit?

my manifesto: reject games that are obvious and make sense upon first glance. make games that can help us experience new and interesting ways to be wrong.
