one of my favorite games is [the beginner's guide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Beginner%27s_Guide). made by the creator of iconic metanarrative [the stanley parable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Stanley_Parable), it twists the medium of games in a fascinating way. the game is narrated by a fictionalized version of its actual creator, davey wreden, who tours you through a series of unfinished games supposedly made by his friend coda. these turn out to be less of games with, you know, win conditions, and more like surreal walking simulators—in fact, davey will sometimes just tell you the answers to the puzzles you encounter. yet the game eventually breaks your understanding of both its world and its characters in a way i won't spoil, but highly recommend experiencing yourself (especially if you enjoy [metafiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metafiction)).

when i tell people i like games, they're sometimes surprised that i never mean triple-a action titles or first-person shooters. instead, i'm a real puzzle gamer. i grew up on [point-and-click escape rooms](https://archive.org/details/adventure-jinx-minx-tower-escape), social deduction games like [town of salem](https://www.townofsalem.io/), [deck-builders](https://dominion.games/), [auto-battlers](https://teamfighttactics.leagueoflegends.com/en-us/). while the beginner's guide is not categorized as traditional "puzzle game", i enjoy it for a very similar reason: it entices you to a specific worldview, then tears it apart.

a good puzzle designer is like a teacher. [baba is you](https://store.steampowered.com/app/736260/Baba_Is_You/) is a great puzzle game where you can interact with programming-like logic blocks. it starts you off with easy tutorial levels that establish rules like "FLAG IS WIN," where the solution is straightforward and obvious. quickly, it ramps up into its _non-obvious_ puzzles. here, the first solution always fails (there must be some head-hitting to build up to a euphoric "a-ha" moment), and you must question your understanding of the game's rules and mechanics to move forward.

![baba is you](/text/120625-puzzles/baba.webp)
> tutorial level from [baba is you](https://store.steampowered.com/app/736260/Baba_Is_You/)

[portal](https://store.steampowered.com/app/400/Portal/) does this particularly well. you first learn the obvious affordance of portals: walk through a blue portal and come out the orange portal, and vice versa. later, you're introduced to walls you can't shoot portals on. you must exploit the existence of gravity itself, falling through looping portals to build enough momentum to fling yourself across the map. your understanding of the rules has expanded.

research in metacognition helps explain why this design can be so effective. [studies on prediction error](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959475217303468?via%3Dihub) and the [hypercorrection effect](https://psycnet.apa.org/doiLanding?doi=10.1037%2F0278-7393.27.6.1491) suggest that errors can become particularly memorable if we were confident we were right. but to produce that kind of useful surprise, we're required to start from a place the player might be confident; that is, the edge of the player's original understanding (or molding some kind of understanding yourself with tutorial levels). if the gap between what the player knows and what the puzzle demands is too large, the lesson will get lost. players will look up the solution to the puzzle.

here, ai in puzzle games introduces a strange new possibility. traditional level designers must anticipate how the imagined average player would react to their puzzles through playtesting; if most people don't get a certain solution, it can't be a core part of the game. but what if we could model the player's expectations and react to them on demand?

at neurips 2025 this past december, i talked with a posterer about _adversarial optimization algorithms_: the idea that you can train game-playing agent (learner) using a second adversarial agent (baddie). say learner is playing overcooked and begins to win games merely by moving around the map clockwise. baddie moves counter-clockwise, blocking learner in a corner until it can learn counter-clockwise navigation. the interesting part is that the adversary can't just sabotage the task; it has to find a _rational adversarial example_, that is, a strategy that makes sense on its own, but happens to expose a weakness in the learner's policy. even with machines, the ideal adversary is one that watches its opponent's strategy and adapts accordingly.

at the same neurips, i exhibited my game [princi/pal](https://cnnmon.itch.io/principal) in one of the [creative ai](https://neurips.cc/Conferences/2025/CallForCreativeAI) corners. princi/pal is a moral dilemma tamagotchi; that is, a virtual pet that asks you for advice on moral dilemmas, learning and growing from the text you provide. over time, the pet becomes increasingly autonomous, questioning advice that seems vague or pushing back when your answers contradict things you've told it before. at the exhibition i watched a surprising thing happen: people enjoyed arguing with their pets. what was initially the result of brittle prompt engineering with gpt-4o-mini led to pets being quite annoyingly insistent on their beliefs, to the point of rarely giving up. players sometimes fell into conversational death spirals where they needed to find the magic words to get their pet to relent, otherwise giving up and letting the pet do as it wanted.

![poster](/text/120625-puzzles/poster.jpg)

i suspect part of the appeal was simply that people don't expect ai, much less a tamagotchi, to fight back with them like a bratty teenager. but i would argue that it was also because the resistance forced players to re-assess their rationale, and question whether what they were saying was actually reasonable, consistent, or clear advice given the circumstances. their straightforward solution—whatever they felt like saying intuitively—was rejected. an ai in real-time can push a player in a way that feels both personalized and challenging. of course, its own understanding can be exploitable and biased (many jailbreaking attempts [worked](https://huggingface.co/datasets/cnnmon/moral-dilemma-responses)). the result is something like two imperfect models poking holes in each other.

the obvious application for ai puzzles exists in education. if a curriculum can continually adjust to a student's weak spots and live inside their [zone of proximal development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zone_of_proximal_development), more students can begin to find education meaningful, not just ones that fit the average student archetype. i'm personally interested in taking this a bit further, to see whether puzzles can help us make sense of messier human systems that we barely even understand, like how we communicate, self-organize, and make decisions. can we make a puzzle game that tackles something like emotional awareness, or online discourse? that notice the assumptions we bring and try to break them?

reject games that are obvious and make sense upon first glance. make games that can help us experience new and interesting ways to be wrong.
